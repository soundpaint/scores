# The Whole World

Due to unclear copyright situation in question, I publish this choral
score – the arrangement is my own piece of work from 1997 – only _as
source code_ in the `LilyPond`[^7] language: It turned out only many
years after authoring this choral score, that the tune is from a
contemporary composer, rather than on a traditional folk tune, as I
was originally told (and hence believed).  For details, read the story
behind this piece of work.

## The Story Behind This Piece of Work

In late August of 1997, I partcipated in a choir trip through Israel.
Our tour guide was a very enthusiastic and friendly whoman.  If I
recall correctly, her name was _Esther_.  As she told, the year before
she had guided members of a sports team that later got involved into
an attack.  So, when we travelled through the Golan Heights with the
bus that we had chartered, she advised us, that in case of an attack
we should just duck and cover, while the bus driver would then bring
us away and to safety as fast as possible.  I felt unsure, if she was
just joking or really meant it.  Luckily, no attack happened, so that
it is now possible for me to write these lines.

To make the long bus ride more comfortable, and since we were all
members of forementioned choir, Esther came up with the idea to teach
us a famous _traditional folk tune_ (as she believed), called “Kol
Ha'olam Kulo” (English: “ The whole world_”).  As a non-professional
singer, her vocal performance was somewhat limited (especially as it
came to rhythmical exactness, but also some melodic details).  She
repeated singing the tune, until we were roughly able to reproduce it,
but each time with slightly variations in the tune and in rhythmic
details.  I took out a small sheet of paper and tried to catch up and
scribbled down versions of this tune and a phonetical transcription of
the Hebrew lyrics that I believed to percieve, just as far as I was
able.  Esther also provided us with a rough translation of the Hebrew
lyrics into German.

Later on the tour, we had a longer stay in Haifa, with a stop on a
small plateau with an overwhelming and inspiring view over parts of
Haifa and its harbour.  The stop went for, maybe, an hour or so, such
that I digged out my scribbeled down notes and a pencil and first
tried to reconstruct the original tune, as I thought it should have
been musically correct.  Still sitting on that plateau in the warm
evening sun with the view over the harbour where the Exodus had been
landing almost exactly 50 years ago (as I learned afterwards), the
next step I felt to do was to create a choral score, based on my
reconstructed version of this tune and the phonetically notated
syllables that I had taken, and the rough translation of the lytics
into German that Esther had provided us with.  I wrote down the
complete arrangement for three female and three male voices while
still sitting on that plateau with only a piece of paper and a pencil.
After the tour, when back in Germany, I tried to correct some minor
suspected mistakes mostly in the second part of the piece.

This piece of work was also the original reason that I got into
contact with the `LilyPond` notation software after rather frustrating
experiences that I have had before with `MusicTeX`.  It was the very
first piece of work that I used `LilyPond` for in late 1997.  Yet, at
that time, the lyrics in my choral score was still based on my own
phonetic transcription of the lyrics as I had it perceived from
listening to Esther, which I felt was a somewhat unsatisfying
situation.  But at that time, the internet was still too young to
provide any useful information on this topic.  Only many years later,
with `LilyPond` emerging into a professional notation software and
increasingly musical resources to be found on the internet, I came up
with the idea of validating my transliteration of the lyrics, and also
verifying my score against the original traditional folk tune, as I
still was believing.  With some research on the internet based on
fragments of my self-made transliteration, I finally found various
resources on the tune, that also made me find more about its origins.

Well, it turned out that what I had believed to be a traditional folk
tune, was in fact a contemporary composition[^1] by a _Rabbi Baruch
Chait_ (*1946)[^2] and made popular probably even more by song writer
_Ofra Haza_ (1957-2000)[^3].  The lyrics

כל העולם כולו<br />
גשר צר מאוד<br />
והעיקר, והעיקר<br />
לא לפחד, לא לפחד כלל<br />

may actually be traced back to words of _Nachman of Breslov_
(1772-1810)[^4], as further research revealed.  While I had mostly
correctly reconstructed the first part of the tune and even the
chords[^5] of my choral score roughly match those of the pop song
(though I had not at all known of this pop song before), especially
the rhythm of the second part clearly differs, even down to what
syllables are emphasized.  That is, when actually performing the
choral score with the original Hebrew lyrics, it may be legimitate to
slightly correct the rhythm to better match the original lyrics.
Beside that, I think, the contrast between the first part with an
almost intimate mood, and the second, rather extroverting part, gets
clear in both, the original pop song, as well as already in my
original choral score version, even though I had no knowledge at all
of the pop song or any other performance of this song, and my score
was based solely on the reconstructed tune and the rough German
translation of the lyrics that Esther provided us with.

The lyrics are, as I learned from various sources on the internet,
very similar to the words of _Nachman of Breslov_, and also extremely
short, not longer than an average citation, such that I rather doubt
that the lyrics deserve copyright.  Yet, the tune is surely still
copyrighted.  Therefore, I provide just my choral score only as
`LilyPond` source code soley for information, rather than as a
ready-to-print PDF file of the score.  You may compile the
`LilyPond`source code into a printable PDF file at your own legal
responsibility if you are sure to obey whatever law applies in your
country.

In case the copyright situation gets clear in the sense that I am
confident to legally publish my score, I will gladly provide the score
as a compiled binary PDF file.
